How to help someone with phone addiction

To help someone with phone addiction, start with a calm and private talk. Describe what you see without blame, and offer support for a change that they choose. Pressure and control usually make people defend the habit. This page shows what to say, what to avoid and how to keep helping over time.

The main rule: support, do not control

You cannot make an adult change their phone habits. You can only make change easier and more attractive. People change when they see a reason for themselves. If they feel attacked or watched, they often defend the habit and hide it.

So your role is that of a friend, not a judge. Show care, share what you notice, and help with the practical parts. Leave the decision to them. This is true for a partner, a friend, a sibling or an adult child. For children and teenagers, read screen addiction in teenagers and screen addiction in children.

Signs that someone may need help

Many people use their phones a lot without a real problem. Look for harm, not only for hours. Some signs that phone use may be a problem:

  • They often use the phone longer than they planned, and they say they regret it.
  • Their sleep, work, study or health suffers.
  • They get irritable or anxious when they cannot use the phone.
  • They stop doing things that they used to enjoy.
  • They use the phone during conversations, meals or while they drive.
  • They tried to cut back but could not.

The page on signs of phone addiction lists more. If the person uses their phone in a way that hurts your relationship, read about phubbing too.

How to start the conversation

  1. Choose a calm time. Do not start the talk in an argument, or when they are on the phone.
  2. Talk in private. Criticism in front of others makes people defensive.
  3. Start with care. Say why you raise the topic, for example "I miss our evenings together."
  4. Describe, do not label. Say what you saw: "You were on your phone until 2 AM three nights this week." Avoid "You are addicted."
  5. Ask, then listen. Ask how they feel about their phone use. Let them talk without interruption.
  6. Offer help, not a plan. Ask what would help them. Offer to try a change together.

Things to avoid

  • Do not take or hide their phone without their agreement.
  • Do not check their phone or install monitoring apps on an adult's phone. That damages trust and can be illegal.
  • Do not compare them with other people.
  • Do not raise the topic every day. One good talk is better than many small fights.
Look at your own habits first. If you also use your phone a lot, say so. "We can both try this" is easier to hear than "you need to change". Parents can read parents phone addiction for more about this.

What to say: examples

The words you choose matter. Here are some examples of calm, clear sentences.

  • "I like it when we talk at dinner. Can we keep the phones away at meals?"
  • "You told me you feel tired in the mornings. Do you think the late phone use is part of it?"
  • "I want to use my phone less too. Do you want to try it together for a week?"
  • "If you want help with the settings, I can sit with you."

Each example describes a fact or a feeling, asks a question, and leaves the choice open. None of them uses a label.

How to support change over time

If the person decides to cut back, your support in the next weeks matters more than the first talk.

  • Make shared phone-free times. Meals, walks or the first hour after work are good places to start.
  • Offer things to do. Suggest a game, a film, a walk or a shared hobby. A gap with no plan pulls people back to the phone. See things to do instead of scrolling.
  • Help with the setup. Offer to help them set limits, turn off notifications or move apps off the home screen. Let them choose the rules.
  • Notice progress. Say when you see a change. Small praise helps more than a reminder of failures.
  • Be patient with slips. A bad week is normal. Read relapse after a phone detox to understand what helps.

When to suggest professional help

Sometimes heavy phone use is part of a bigger problem. A person may use the phone to escape anxiety, low mood, loneliness or stress. In that case, limits alone may not help, and the feelings may get stronger when the phone goes away.

This page is general information, not medical advice. Phone use can cause serious problems in someone's life, or come with signs of strong anxiety or depression. In that case, gently suggest that they talk to a doctor or a mental health professional. If you think someone is in danger or may harm themselves, contact local crisis support or emergency services now.

Questions

How do I tell someone they are addicted to their phone?

Avoid the word addicted. Choose a calm, private moment, start with why you care, and describe what you saw, such as late nights on the phone. Then ask how they feel about it and listen.

Can I force someone to stop using their phone?

You cannot force an adult to change, and pressure often makes people defend the habit. You can make change easier: share what you notice, offer support, create phone-free times together and help with the setup if they ask.

Should I install a monitoring app on my partner's phone?

No. Monitoring an adult's phone without their agreement damages trust and can be illegal. Tools to reduce phone use work best when the person chooses them and sets their own rules.

What if the person does not want to change?

Respect their choice, and keep the relationship warm. You can set rules for shared times, such as no phones at meals, and talk about it again later. People often change when they find their own reason.

When should someone get professional help for phone addiction?

Suggest professional help if phone use causes serious problems in their life, or if they show signs of strong anxiety or depression. If you think someone is in danger, contact local crisis support or emergency services now.
