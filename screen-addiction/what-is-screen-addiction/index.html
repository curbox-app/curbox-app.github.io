Screen addiction · Definition

What is screen addiction?

Screen addiction is a pattern of screen use that you find hard to control and that causes problems in your life. It is a common term, not a formal diagnosis. This page explains what it means, how it differs from normal use, and what you can do about it.

Screen addiction: a short definition

Screen addiction is compulsive use of phones, tablets, computers or TVs that continues even when it harms your sleep, work, health or relationships. The key word is control. A person with a screen addiction often wants to use less, tries to use less, and then uses more than planned.

The amount of time alone does not define it. Two people can spend the same hours on a screen. One uses it for work and calls with family. The other loses hours in feeds they did not want to open. The second pattern is closer to what most people mean by screen addiction.

Many people use the words "phone addiction" or "smartphone addiction" for the same idea, because the phone is the screen that is always with you. Researchers often prefer the term "problematic smartphone use", because it describes the behavior without a medical label.

Is screen addiction a real diagnosis?

Not as a single condition. The DSM-5, which many clinicians use, does not list smartphone addiction or screen addiction as a diagnosis. It lists Internet Gaming Disorder only as a condition for more study.

The World Health Organization does include "gaming disorder" in the ICD-11. That diagnosis covers games only. It describes poor control over gaming, a higher priority for gaming than for other activities, and continued gaming despite harm.

So the science is still in progress. Even so, the problem is real for many people. You do not need a diagnosis to decide that your screen time does not match your goals. If you want to see where you stand, try the phone addiction self-check.

Normal screen use versus screen addiction

Screens are a normal part of life. You use them to work, learn, pay bills, talk to friends and relax. Heavy use is not always a problem. These four questions can help you see the difference:

  1. Control. Can you stop when you planned to stop?
  2. Priority. Do screens push out sleep, meals, exercise or people?
  3. Harm. Does your use cause problems at work, at school or at home?
  4. Feelings. Do you feel restless, anxious or irritable when you cannot use your phone?

If you answer "yes" to one question now and then, that is common. If you answer "yes" to several of them most days, your screen use is probably more than a habit. The page on signs of phone addiction lists more examples.

Common types of screen addiction

Most people do not struggle with "screens" in general. They struggle with one or two kinds of content. When you know your type, you can target it.

  • Social media. Feeds, likes and comments. See social media addiction.
  • Short-form video. Reels, Shorts and TikTok, where the next video starts on its own.
  • Mobile games. Games with daily rewards, streaks and in-game purchases.
  • Binge-watching. Series and long videos with autoplay.
  • News and forums. Endless updates, often about bad news.
  • Compulsive checking. Short, frequent checks of messages, email or notifications.

Each type has its own pull, but they share a design pattern: no natural stop point and a reward that arrives at random. The guide on why phones are so addictive explains this in more detail.

What causes screen addiction?

There is rarely one cause. Usually several factors work together:

  • App design. Infinite scroll, autoplay and notifications make it easy to start and hard to stop.
  • Reward learning. Your brain learns that the phone sometimes gives something good. The dopamine and phone addiction page covers this.
  • Mood. Many people reach for the phone when they feel bored, stressed, lonely or tired.
  • Access. The phone is always in your pocket, so the habit has no barrier.

Age also matters. Children and teenagers have less developed self-control, so app design can affect them more. Read more about screen addiction in teenagers and screen addiction in children.

First steps if you think you have a screen addiction

You do not need to quit your phone. The goal is control, not zero use. Start small:

  1. Measure your real screen time for one week. Do not change anything yet.
  2. Find the one app or activity that takes the most time.
  3. Turn off notifications from that app.
  4. Add a pause before that app opens.
  5. Set one daily limit for it.
  6. Choose one activity to do in that time instead.

General information only. This page is not medical advice. If phone use causes serious problems in your life, talk to a doctor or a mental health professional. If you feel unsafe or think about self-harm, contact local crisis support or emergency services now.

Questions

What is screen addiction in simple words?

Screen addiction is when you use screens more than you want to and find it hard to stop. The use also causes problems with sleep, work, health or relationships. It is a common term, not a formal medical diagnosis.

Is screen addiction the same as phone addiction?

Mostly, yes. Screen addiction covers all screens, such as phones, tablets, computers and TVs. Phone addiction or smartphone addiction is the most common form, because the phone is always with you.

Is screen addiction a medical diagnosis?

No. The DSM-5 does not list smartphone addiction or screen addiction. The World Health Organization lists gaming disorder in the ICD-11, but that covers games only.

How many hours of screen time is an addiction?

There is no fixed number. The amount of time matters less than control and harm. If you cannot stop when you plan to, that is a warning sign. If your use also hurts your sleep, work or relationships, the number of hours does not matter.

Can you recover from screen addiction?

Yes. Most people can get back control with small changes. Measure your use, remove triggers such as notifications, add friction, and set limits. Then replace the habit with something you value. If it causes serious problems, talk to a doctor or mental health professional.
