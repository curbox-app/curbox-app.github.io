The screen time app that shows you ads is working for someone else.

Why an ad-funded blocker is an odd thing to trust with your screen, and a five-minute audit you can run on any app before you install it.

By the Curbox team · Updated 22 November 2026

Search the Play Store for a screen time app and you get dozens of results with the same promise: take back your time, stop scrolling, focus. Install a few and a pattern shows up quickly. A banner at the bottom of the usage chart. A full-screen video ad when you open settings. The one feature you actually wanted, a lock you cannot talk your way out of, sitting behind a monthly subscription.

Some of that is just how free apps pay for themselves. But a screen time app is not a flashlight or a calculator. To do its job it needs more access to your phone than almost anything else you will install, and that changes the math on who you let build it.

What a blocker can see

On Android, nearly every app blocker works through the Accessibility Service. It is the only reliable way for one app to know which other app is on screen at this moment, so it can draw a block screen over it. Website blocking goes further: the blocker reads your browser's address bar to know which page you are on.

Accessibility access was designed for screen readers, so it is broad on purpose. An app holding it can read the text on your screen, including messages, and observe what you type. Pair that with the "display over other apps" permission and the app can draw anything on top of anything. Every blocker needs this pair. Curbox needs it too. That is not the problem.

The question is never whether a blocker has this access. It is who else is inside the app with it.

Why ads change the picture

Ads in an Android app do not arrive by magic. The developer adds an advertising SDK, a library written by another company, and that code runs inside the app, with the app's permissions. It typically ships along with analytics and attribution libraries that report device details, app usage and interactions back to their own servers. For an app with Accessibility access, that means third-party code you have never seen is running in the same process that can see your screen.

That does not mean a given ad SDK is reading your messages. Reputable ones have policies against it. It means you are now trusting the developer, plus every company whose code they bundled, plus whatever those libraries do in their next update. You cannot check any of it.

There is also a quieter conflict. An app paid by ad impressions earns more when you open it more often and stay in it longer. A blocker's whole purpose is to get you to spend less time on your phone, including in the blocker. The incentives point in opposite directions. The same is true, in a softer way, of apps where the strict mode is the premium tier: the version that actually works is the one you have to keep paying for.

Fair is fair Plenty of developers use ads or subscriptions honestly and need the income. This page is not about naming and shaming. It is about knowing what to check, so you can decide for yourself.

What "no ads" should actually mean

"Ad-free" on a store listing can mean anything from "no banners in the paid tier" to "we never added an ad SDK at all." The useful version is stricter: no advertising code, no third-party tracking code, and ideally no way for the app to reach the internet in the first place. If an app has no internet permission, it physically cannot send what it sees to anyone, ads or not. That is a guarantee the operating system enforces, not a promise in a privacy policy.

The checklist below takes about five minutes and works on any app, including ours.

Audit any screen time app in 5 minutes

Tick as you go

  1. On the Play Store, open the listing, scroll to "About this app", then "App permissions" at the bottom. On F-Droid the list is on the app page. Look for "have full network access" or "INTERNET". A blocker that holds Accessibility and also has internet access can, in principle, send anything it sees. It might have a good reason, like sync. Note it and move on.

    Red flag: internet access with no feature that explains it.

  2. Go to reports.exodus-privacy.eu.org and search the app name or package id. Exodus scans the app file for known tracker signatures, such as ad networks, analytics and crash reporters, and lists every permission it declares. Zero trackers is a good sign. Several ad or analytics trackers in an app that wants Accessibility access is worth a long pause. Exodus only spots trackers it knows, so a clean report is strong evidence, not proof.

    Red flag: advertising or analytics trackers listed.

  3. Look for a link to a code repository (GitHub, GitLab, Codeberg) and a license such as GPL or MIT. Being listed on F-Droid is a strong signal, because F-Droid builds apps from their published source, does not accept proprietary libraries such as ad SDKs, and labels tracking with an "Anti-Feature" warning. Public code means anyone can check what the app does with its access. Our open-source app blocker guide covers how to verify this properly.

    Red flag: "privacy-first" claims with no code to back them.

  4. Install it and walk through the first screens. Does it ask for an email or a Google sign-in before you can block a single app? An account ties your usage data to your identity on someone else's server. Optional accounts for sync are reasonable. Mandatory ones for a tool that could work entirely on your phone deserve a question: why do they need to know who you are?

    Red flag: sign-up required before any feature works.

  5. Open the upgrade screen and read it carefully. The features that matter most in a blocker are the ones that hold when you are tempted: a strict mode you cannot switch off, protection against uninstalling, schedules, website blocking. If those are all premium, the free version is a demo. That can be a fair deal, but know what you are signing up for, and check how cancellation works.

    Red flag: the "stay blocked" part only works if you pay monthly.

Questions

Is there a screen time app for Android with no ads and no subscription?

Yes. Curbox is free, has no ads, no subscription and no account, and its full source code is public under the GPL v3. Several other open-source blockers are also ad-free; see the open-source app blocker guide.

How can I tell if an app has trackers?

Search for the app on Exodus Privacy (reports.exodus-privacy.eu.org). It scans the app's installable file for known tracker code and lists its permissions. A clean report is a good sign, though it only detects trackers it already knows about.

Why does an app blocker need Accessibility access?

To see which app is on screen so it can show a block screen, and to read things like the browser address bar for website blocking. That access can see everything on your screen, which is why it matters who wrote the app and whether you can read its code.

Does Curbox connect to the internet?

No. The Curbox Android app has no internet permission in any variant, so it cannot send anything anywhere. The optional browser extension only connects for things you turn on, such as pairing with your phone. Details are in the privacy policy.

Is a free app with ads always bad?

Not always. But a blocker is a special case: it holds one of the most powerful permissions on Android, and ad code runs inside the same app. If you can, pick one that does not need ads to pay its bills.
